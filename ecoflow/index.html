Featured project

EcoFlow - Industrial Wastewater Treatment Analytics

This dashboard monitors an imaginary wastewater treatment plant. A computer model makes up 120 days of realistic data; the dashboard then shows how well the plant cleans the water, and what that costs in electricity and chemicals.

Engineering insights

Most of the time the simulated plant removes about 85 to 92% of COD, 90 to 96% of BOD and over 90% of suspended solids, which is what a healthy conventional treatment plant should manage. The most useful story in the charts is the upsets: on those days the incoming water is no dirtier than usual, but much more pollution gets through. That tells you the problem is in the treatment process itself, not in what is arriving at the plant.

Three things matter when reading the numbers. First, the removal figures compare the concentration going in with the concentration going out, and the model assumes the same amount of water leaves as enters; a real plant loses some water along the way, which changes the sums. Second, obviously wrong sensor readings are removed before anything is calculated, because a single stuck sensor would otherwise drag a whole day's average. Third, the improved-settings scenario is a what-if model, not a measurement: its savings follow directly from the assumptions put into it.

A dashboard like this can point an engineer at where to look, but it cannot make the decision. Before changing anything on a real plant you would also need the legal discharge limits, laboratory samples to double-check the online sensors, the design capacity of each treatment step, and the safety rules around chemical dosing.

The what-if scenario models two specific changes: speed-controlled motors on the air blowers (less electricity per m³ of water) and more careful coagulant dosing (less chemical, slightly better solids removal). The numbers are modelled estimates built on the assumptions listed below. They are not measured results, and no real-world savings, costs or regulatory compliance are claimed.

    Metric definitions and units

    Removal efficiency (%)

    How much of a pollutant the plant takes out: (in − out) ÷ in × 100, using each day's average concentrations. 90% means only a tenth of the pollution gets through. The model assumes the same amount of water leaves as enters, so this simple formula holds.

    COD / BOD / TSS (mg/L)

    Standard measures of how dirty water is. COD and BOD both measure organic pollution (BOD is the part bacteria can break down, so it is always below COD); TSS measures the solid particles floating in the water.

    Water treated (m³)

    The total water that flowed through the plant in the chosen dates, in cubic metres. One m³ is 1,000 litres.

    Electricity per m³ (kWh/m³)

    Electricity used divided by water treated. It answers a simple question: how much energy does one m³ of cleaned water cost?

    Chemical dose (g/m³)

    Coagulant is a chemical that makes tiny particles clump together so they settle out of the water. This is the grams of it used per m³ treated. Illustrative only, never dosing advice.

    Pollutant load (kg/day)

    Flow × concentration: the actual mass of pollutant entering or leaving per day. Concentration alone can mislead when the flow changes, so the dataset reports mass too.

    Data cleaning

    Sensor readings that are clearly wrong (spikes, stuck values, missing or impossible numbers) are removed before anything on this page is calculated. The bars on the water quality chart show what was removed.

    Scenarios

    Normal running is the plant as it is, bad days included. Improved settings applies the two what-if changes to exactly the same days, so the comparison is fair.
