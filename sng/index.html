Synthetic Natural Gas Plant Design

A process-design case study for a plant making 22,000 tonnes of synthetic natural gas a year from biomass-derived syngas. My final-year group project was on this topic; this study is rebuilt from scratch as an educational exercise, with every assumption written down and every calculation tested.

Engineering insights

Methanation is strongly exothermic: most of the engineering effort in a real SNG plant goes into removing that heat safely and using it well. In this model the methanation cooling duty dwarfs every other duty, which is why the energy chart is dominated by one bar.

The shift reactor is the lever that sets everything downstream, and the design case deliberately shows what happens when it is set naively. Targeting three hydrogen molecules per carbon monoxide molecule sounds right, but it leaves nothing for the carbon dioxide also in the gas, so CO₂ passes through unconverted. The balanced-ratio scenario retargets the shift so hydrogen covers both reactions, and the CO₂ in the product falls from about 8 to 1 mol%. The twist is that overall purity barely moves, because leftover hydrogen and inert nitrogen take CO₂'s place. Finding the shortfall, fixing it, and then seeing why the fix alone is not enough (a real plant adds a recycle or membrane step) is the main lesson of this study.

The aged-catalyst scenario shows why plants monitor conversion closely: a drop from 98% to 90% CO conversion cuts both the production rate and the product purity, and unreacted CO in the product is a quality and safety problem for a gas grid.

What this model leaves out matters as much as what it includes: no reaction kinetics or reactor sizing, no recycle streams, no pressure-drop or compressor staging calculations, and no costs. The documentation lists every simplification.
