Project 04 · Urban mobility · Data analytics · Sustainability

What if we worked closer to home?

An evidence-based investigation into Kuala Lumpur's congestion, commuting patterns and sustainable mobility alternatives. The starting question is a hypothesis, not a conclusion: would traffic improve if more people lived and worked in the same region?

Research overview

Greater Kuala Lumpur concentrates jobs while much of its workforce lives further and further out, and the region keeps adding cars. This project asks whether the congestion that follows is mainly a geography problem (where people live versus where they work), a transport problem (what the alternatives to driving are like), or both, and what the open data can and cannot say about it.

The evidence base is deliberately public and reproducible: daily public transport ridership since 2019, a full year of rail station origin-destination counts, car registration transactions, population and employment series, the national migration survey, and cited congestion metrics. Where the data runs out, the gaps are stated rather than filled in. A separate simulator explores what-if scenarios under adjustable assumptions, and its outputs are always labelled as modelled.

Counterarguments and limitations

What the evidence supports. Rail flows point strongly at the city centre, the weekday-weekend gap shows commuting dominates rail use, Selangor keeps growing while KL's resident population is flat, and KL's published congestion metrics worsened year on year. Together these are consistent with long inward commutes being a real feature of the region.

What it does not support. None of this proves that moving jobs closer to homes would cut congestion by any particular amount. Induced demand is the classic counterargument: road space freed by one driver tends to be taken by another. Decentralising jobs can also lengthen some commutes (people do not relocate neatly), and remote work can increase non-work driving. The migration survey measures where people move house, not where they commute, and most stated reasons for moving are family-related, not career-related.

Competing explanations for congestion include fuel and parking prices, vehicle affordability, land-use rules, the quality and reliability of buses (for which no open travel-time data exists), and simple population growth. The data here cannot rank these causes.

The decisive gaps. Malaysia publishes no open door-to-door commuting matrix, no jobs-by-workplace geography at useful detail, and no open road traffic counts. Until those exist, the honest verdict on the hypothesis is: plausible, partially supported, unproven.

Where the question stands

Supported

Commuting dominates rail use; flows point at the centre; the region's population grows outward while KL's is flat; published congestion metrics worsened.

Inconclusive

Whether shorter home-to-work distances would reduce congestion, and by how much. The commuting and traffic data needed to test this are not open.

Contradicts the simple story

Most residential moves are family-driven, not job-driven, and KL is a net sender of residents even while it remains the destination of rail flows.

Hypothetical only

Every simulator output. They show the arithmetic scale of the levers, not predicted outcomes.

Open questions

Actual commute origin-destination patterns, bus travel times, workplace geography, and the effect of the new rail lines opening since 2026.
